### Title
Identity encryption public key makes DKG shares decryptable by anyone - ([File: crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs])

### Summary

`EncryptionKeyMessage::read` accepts the canonical identity point as a participant’s encryption public key. A malicious participant can register that key, causing every sender to derive the ECDH result as the public identity point and encrypt that participant’s secret shares with a publicly derivable ChaCha20 key.

### Finding Description

`EncryptionKeyMessage::read` reads `enc_key` with `C::read_G` but performs no non-identity or possession check. [1](#0-0)  `Ciphersuite::read_G` only requires successful decoding and canonical round-tripping, so the identity encoding is accepted. [2](#0-1)  For Ristretto, `from_bytes` accepts decompressed points and the torsion-free predicate is unconditional, including identity. [3](#0-2) 

During share generation, the sender computes `ecdh(key, to)`, where `key` is a fresh ephemeral scalar and `to` is the registered recipient public key. [4](#0-3)  If `to` is identity, `ecdh` returns identity regardless of the secret ephemeral scalar. [5](#0-4)  The cipher derives its ChaCha20 key solely from the public context and that ECDH result, then uses a fixed IV. [6](#0-5)  Consequently, anyone possessing the ciphertext can reproduce the keystream and recover the plaintext share bytes.

### Impact Explanation

An observer of the authenticated DKG transport can recover every secret share addressed to a participant that registered the identity encryption key. Those plaintext shares are confidential DKG material, and their disclosure can contribute toward reconstruction or misuse of the participant’s resulting threshold share if enough related material is exposed. [7](#0-6)  The protection specifically exists to prevent share exposure during communication, so encrypting to a universally computable key defeats the protocol’s confidentiality guarantee. [8](#0-7) 

### Likelihood Explanation

A participating DKG party controls its serialized `EncryptionKeyMessage` and can replace the trailing `enc_key` encoding with the canonical identity encoding while retaining otherwise valid commitments. [9](#0-8)  Receivers register the supplied value without checking that it is non-identity or that its owner knows a corresponding nonzero secret. [10](#0-9)  Exploitation requires visibility of the encrypted share traffic, but the encryption layer exists precisely because those messages may traverse an authenticated transport that does not otherwise guarantee confidentiality.

### Recommendation

Reject identity—and preferably any encoding that cannot be shown to correspond to a nonzero scalar—for `EncryptionKeyMessage::enc_key`. The validation can occur in `Decryption::register`, before inserting `msg.enc_key`, and should be mirrored by any standalone commitment-message processing. A proof of possession for the long-term encryption key would also prevent participants from registering identity or another party’s public key.

### Proof of Concept

Conceptually, for `Ristretto`:

```rust
// Obtain a normally generated EncryptionKeyMessage<_, Commitments<_>>.
let mut msg = honest_commitment_message.serialize();

// Ristretto's canonical identity encoding is 32 zero bytes.
let len = msg.len();
msg[len - 32 ..].copy_from_slice(&[0u8; 32]);

// Peers accept this message because read_G accepts canonical identity.
let malicious =
  EncryptionKeyMessage::<Ristretto, Commitments<Ristretto>>::read(
    &mut msg.as_slice(),
    params,
  ).unwrap();
```

When a sender encrypts a share to this participant, `ecdh::<Ristretto>(&ephemeral, malicious.enc_key)` is the identity point. [11](#0-10)  The observer can therefore derive the same ChaCha20 stream from the public context and identity encoding, XOR it with `msg.msg`, and recover the serialized `SecretShare`. [6](#0-5)

```markdown
### Encryption

In order to protect the secret shares during communication, the `dkg` library
establishes a public key for encryption at the start of a given protocol.
Every encrypted message (such as the secret shares) then includes a per-message
encryption key. These two keys are used in an Elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman
handshake to derive a shared key. This shared key is then hashed to obtain a key
and IV for use in a ChaCha20 stream cipher instance, which is xor'd against a
message to encrypt it.
```
