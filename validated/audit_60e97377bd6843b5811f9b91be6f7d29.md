## Analysis

The M-5 report's core broken invariant: a system **commits to honor an unbounded, growing set of promises (accrued rewards) without any capacity check or expiry**, so promises made later can silently invalidate/displace promises made earlier, and the party that already paid loses what they paid for.

The closest verifiable local analog is `pallet-bandwidth`'s prepaid subscription ledger, not the reward-claim pallets (those transfer immediately from a treasury and simply fail the extrinsic if underfunded — no silent loss of a *different* party's already-paid-for entitlement).

### Title
Bandwidth subscription FIFO eviction lets a permissionless purchaser destroy another payer's already-paid, unexpired allowance - ([File: modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs])

### Summary
`pallet-bandwidth` stores each `(app_chain, app)` pair's prepaid byte allowances as a FIFO `BoundedVec<Subscription, 1024>`. `push_subscription` [1](#0-0)  silently evicts the **oldest** entry once the list hits the 1024 cap, regardless of how many bytes remain unconsumed on it. Purchases are permissionless and chain-independent by design — sponsorship lets "a buyer on Ethereum credit an app on Base" — [2](#0-1)  meaning any account, on any registered source chain, can push a purchase into *any* `(app_chain, app)` bucket without being the app owner or a prior payer for that bucket.

### Finding Description
`BandwidthManager.purchase()` dispatches a credit message that `on_accept` turns into `push_subscription(app_chain, app, tier, bytes, duration_secs)` [3](#0-2) . This function performs no check on whether evicting the head would discard bytes that are still far from expiry, and no restriction correlates the tier/size of the *new* purchase to the tier/size of the entry it displaces: [4](#0-3) 

Because eviction only triggers "once the list is already at 1024 entries," an attacker does not need to fund 1024 purchases from scratch — they only need to push a targeted `(app_chain, app)` bucket that is *already near capacity* (a natural state for any actively-sponsored, popular app) over the cap with a single cheap purchase (the lowest tier, $50/100 KB) to evict the oldest live entry — which could be an expensive, high-byte, long-duration subscription (e.g. the $1000/8 MB tier) that a legitimate sponsor paid for and that has barely been drained. The docs even acknowledge the eviction path is real and "in practice this only happens under pathological repeat-buy behavior" [5](#0-4) , but this dismisses the case where the queue is organically near-full and a single low-cost purchase from an unrelated party is enough to trigger it — the guard that exists (`SubscriptionEvicted` event) is only an audit trail, not a preventive check, exactly mirroring the M-5 pattern of "no check that the contract has enough capacity to fund what it has committed to."

The drain order compounds this: `BandwidthGate::try_consume` always burns from the FIFO head [6](#0-5) , so a large subscription sitting near the head is exposed to eviction risk the whole time it's unconsumed, not just at the end of its life.

### Impact Explanation
This is a direct loss-of-funds vector: the victim paid (via `BandwidthManager.purchase()`, pulling real ERC-20 value on the source chain) for a byte allowance that is destroyed before use by an unrelated, unprivileged party, with no compensation, no minimum stake correlation, and no per-payer isolation. It fits the bounty's "loss of funds" category — value that was properly escrowed/paid is permanently forfeited due to a logic gap in the ledger's capacity accounting, not because of a malicious relayer, prover, or governance actor.

### Likelihood Explanation
Requires only: (1) a target `(app_chain, app)` whose subscription list is near the 1024 cap — plausible for any popular sponsored app receiving frequent top-ups — and (2) one or more cheap `purchase()` calls from any account on a registered source chain. No privileged role, no relayer collusion, no proof forgery needed; it is a pure state-machine/logic issue reachable through the public `purchase()`/`on_accept` entrypoint.

### Recommendation
- Do not evict silently based on FIFO position alone. Either reject the new purchase (`Error::AllowanceListFull`) when the list is full and no entries are expired, forcing the buyer to wait or choosing a different target, or
- Weight eviction eligibility by remaining value (e.g., only evict entries whose `remaining_bytes` is below some dust threshold, or refund evicted purchasers pro-rata from a shared pool), and
- Consider isolating per-payer entries (so one buyer's purchases cannot evict another buyer's paid-for subscription) rather than a single shared FIFO per `(app_chain, app)`.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker (or the app's normal traffic) drives the `(app_chain=EVM-8453, app="MyApp")` subscription list to 1023 entries via repeated cheap purchases (`purchase(TierOne, 1 month)` each ≈ $50).
2. A legitimate sponsor purchases `TierFour` ($1000, 8 MB, 30-day window) — becomes entry #1024, `remaining_bytes = 8_000_000`, `expires_at = now + 30d`.
3. Attacker issues one more `purchase(TierOne, 1 month)` for the same `(app_chain, app)`. `push_subscription` sees `list.len() == MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`, evicts index 0 (the oldest entry — which, depending on ordering, could already be an earlier, still-valuable purchase; repeating steps 1–3 with slight timing lets an attacker specifically target eviction of the just-purchased large entry by controlling insertion order), emits `SubscriptionEvicted { lost_bytes }`.
4. The evicted sponsor's paid $1000 credit is gone; `Allowance::<T>::mutate` in `try_consume`/`push_subscription` never refunds or compensates them [7](#0-6) .

Note: the exact minimal-cost eviction ordering (targeting a *specific* victim entry rather than just "the oldest") is not something I could fully verify from the index alone — confirming the precise FIFO insertion/removal semantics under concurrent purchases would benefit from running the pallet's existing test suite (`modules/pallets/testsuite/src/tests/pallet_bandwidth.rs`) or a Devin session with full repo access, since some file contents may be excluded from this index.

				});
			}

			expires_at
		}

## Sponsorship

The purchase message carries its own `chain` (the _credit chain_) which is **independent of the source chain** that sent the message. This means a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base by dispatching a purchase whose payload sets `chain = "EVM-8453"`.

The pallet keys allowance storage by `(app_chain, app)` taken from the message body, not by `request.source`. The event `BandwidthCredited` carries both — `app_chain` (where the credit lands) and `paid_from` (where the payment came from) — so the cross-chain payer is auditable.

This is what makes the system multi-tenant friendly: a treasury on a single chain can sponsor bandwidth for an app deployed across many chains, without having to deploy `BandwidthManager` on each chain the app lives on.
