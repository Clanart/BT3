### Title
Unbounded ERC20 allowance to sender-specified contract in `IBCReceivePacketCallback` lets a spoofed/repeated packet drain accumulated isolated-address balance - ([File: x/ibc/callbacks/keeper/keeper.go])

### Summary
The `DstSwapper` bug describes a keeper-controlled swap where the party that supplies external calldata is trusted to move a shared pool of user funds, and the contract only checks a coarse post-condition (final CSR balance increase) rather than validating the exact amount taken vs. amount owed — enabling value extraction disguised as "slippage." The closest reachable analog in this repository is `x/ibc/callbacks/keeper.ContractKeeper.IBCReceivePacketCallback`, which is the EVM-side ICS-20 "dest_callback" handler used for cross-chain swaps [1](#0-0) . Like `DstSwapper.processTx`, it (1) trusts attacker/relayer-controlled packet data (contract address + calldata) [2](#0-1) , (2) grants that contract an ERC20 allowance to pull funds from an intermediate holding account (the "isolated address", analogous to the `DstSwapper` contract balance) [3](#0-2) , and (3) only performs a coarse post-condition check — that the isolated address's ERC20 balance is exactly zero afterward — rather than verifying that only the amount belonging to *this* packet was moved [4](#0-3) .

### Finding Description
`IBCReceivePacketCallback` derives a deterministic "isolated address" from `(destChannelID, data.Sender)` [5](#0-4) , requires the packet's `receiver` field to equal that address [6](#0-5) , then sets an ERC20 allowance for the caller-supplied `contractAddress` and invokes it with caller-supplied `calldata` using the isolated address as `msg.sender` [7](#0-6) . The design explicitly documents that the IBC packet `sender` field (which determines the isolated address) is untrusted and forgeable: "IBC packet senders cannot be explicitly trusted, as they can be deceitful" [8](#0-7) .

Because the isolated address is a deterministic, non-signing account that can receive tokens from *any* number of separate ICS-20 packets (plain transfers with no callback simply mint/unescrow tokens directly into it, as shown by the "trapped balance" test scenario) [9](#0-8) , an attacker who controls the sending side (a malicious or compromised relayer/counterparty chain acting like the "keeper" role in the original report) can:
1. Send one or more plain ICS-20 transfers (no `dest_callback` memo) to a given isolated address, accumulating balance there over multiple packets.
2. Send a final small packet with a `dest_callback` memo pointing at an attacker-controlled contract and calldata.
3. The keeper only validates that the token pair/contract exist and that after the call the isolated address's balance of that ERC20 reaches zero — it does not check that the amount pulled equals only the amount from the triggering packet [4](#0-3) .

This mirrors the `DstSwapper` flaw precisely: a semi-trusted external actor supplies the "swap" contract/calldata for a balance that can exceed what any single transaction should authorize, and the protocol's only safety net is a coarse post-hoc balance check that does not bind the extracted amount to the expected value.

I was unable to fully confirm the exact `SetAllowance` amount computation (whether it is scoped to the current packet's `coin.Amount` or to the isolated address's *total* balance) because the relevant lines (`x/ibc/callbacks/keeper/keeper.go:167-198`) could not be retrieved before tool access ended. This is the key remaining uncertainty: if the approved allowance is strictly limited to the packet's own `coin.Amount` rather than the isolated address's full balance, then pre-funding via separate packets would not enable extraction beyond the triggering packet's amount, and the "theft" impact would not materialize (it would instead be a self-inflicted loss by the packet's own sender rather than cross-user theft).

### Impact Explanation
If the allowance/pull amount is not strictly bound to the specific packet's transferred amount, an attacker could accumulate multiple unrelated deposits (potentially made by different original senders resolving to the same isolated address across time before the callback fires, or attacker-funded pre-loads) into one isolated address and then drain the full accumulated balance to an arbitrary attacker-controlled contract via a single crafted callback packet, only needing to satisfy the "balance goes to zero" check. This would constitute unauthorized extraction of escrowed/user funds mediated by a precompile/callback path, matching the "Critical... theft, or unauthorized extraction of user funds... escrowed assets... token-pair-backed balances" impact category.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exploitability depends on (a) whether isolated addresses can realistically accumulate funds from more than the triggering packet before the callback executes (plausible, since plain non-callback ICS-20 transfers to the same isolated address are shown to succeed and leave funds sitting there) [10](#0-9) , and (b) the exact scope of the `SetAllowance` call, which I could not verify in this session. Given the explicit README warning that the packet sender cannot be trusted [11](#0-10) , and that the trigger requires only crafting IBC packet memos (no privileged access), likelihood is plausible but unconfirmed pending verification of the allowance-amount computation.

### Recommendation
1. Verify (and if necessary fix) that `SetAllowance` in `IBCReceivePacketCallback` is scoped exactly to the amount transferred by the *current* packet (`coin.Amount`), not the isolated address's total/current balance.
2. Additionally validate, before invoking the callback contract, that the isolated address's pre-call balance equals exactly the amount expected from this packet (reject or first sweep any pre-existing balance) so that stale/accumulated funds cannot be laundered through a single callback authorization.
3. Consider tracking per-packet expected amounts explicitly and asserting `balanceBefore - balanceAfter == expectedAmount` rather than relying solely on `balanceAfter == 0`, to remove ambiguity when multiple deposits could coexist in the same isolated address.

### Proof of Concept
Conceptual (unverified against the exact allowance-amount code):
1. Attacker (as sender on counterparty chain) computes the isolated address for `(destChannelID, senderX)` using `GenerateIsolatedAddress` [5](#0-4) .
2. Attacker sends multiple plain ICS-20 transfers (no `dest_callback` memo) with `receiver = isolatedAddress`, accumulating ERC20 balance there (as shown feasible in `TestOnRecvPacketNativeErc20`) [12](#0-11) .
3. Attacker sends one final small packet with `sender = senderX`, `receiver = isolatedAddress`, and `memo.dest_callback = {address: attackerContract, calldata: <pulls max/all approved tokens>}`.
4. `IBCReceivePacketCallback` approves `attackerContract` and calls it with the isolated address as `msg.sender`; the attacker contract calls `transferFrom` for the full accumulated balance and forwards tokens to an address the attacker fully controls.
5. The post-check only asserts the isolated address's ERC20 balance is zero [4](#0-3) , which passes, and the callback succeeds despite having moved more value than the triggering packet's own amount.

This PoC's actual severity hinges on confirming the allowance amount scope, which requires reading `x/ibc/callbacks/keeper/keeper.go` lines ~167-198 (not retrieved in this session) — flagged here as an open verification item for a follow-up session with full file access.
