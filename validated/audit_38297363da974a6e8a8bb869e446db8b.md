### Title

PedPoG DKG accepts identity encryption keys, silently turning share "encryption" into a public broadcast — leaks a participant's full set of incoming secret shares to any observer (crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs)

### Summary

The PedPoP DKG encrypts each secret share via ECDH between an ephemeral per-message key and the recipient's registered `enc_key`. `EncryptionKeyMessage::read` and `EncryptedMessage::read` deserialize these keys with `Ciphersuite::read_G`, which only enforces canonicality — unlike `Curve::read_G` in `crypto/frost`, it does not reject the identity point. A participant who registers `enc_key = identity` causes every share sent to them to be encrypted with a publicly computable keystream (`key * identity = identity`), so the ciphertexts are effectively plaintext to any passive observer. This mirrors the Liquid incident's class: protection rendered void, stored/transmitted sensitive data exposed to parties who should not have it.

### Impact Explanation

Every honest participant sends the malicious participant one encrypted share. With `enc_key = identity`, all of those ciphertexts decrypt under a keystream derivable by anyone who knows the (public, derivable) `context`. An unprivileged network observer — who in Serai's threat model can see the broadcast/share messages — recovers all `n` shares `f_i(j)` addressed to participant `j`, i.e. participant `j`'s complete threshold secret share `s_j = Σ f_i(j)`. That is recovery of a key share that the encryption layer exists specifically to protect, directly analogous to Liquid's leakage of protected user data. It reduces the effective security of the threshold key: anyone holding `s_j` needs only `t - 1` further shares (or collusion with `t - 1` participants) to reconstruct the group secret, and it does so without triggering any blame or error path — the DKG completes "successfully".

### Likelihood Explanation

Requires a malicious DKG participant who submits a syntactically valid `EncryptionKeyMessage` with `enc_key = identity` — fully reachable through the public `EncryptionKeyMessage::read` path with no special privileges. However, impact requires an observer positioned to see the share messages (p2p layer), and the leaked share belongs to the attacker's own index, so the gain is weakening the threshold for everyone rather than direct key recovery. Medium severity, matching the source incident's severity.

### Recommendation

Reject the identity point when reading encryption keys: use an identity-checking reader (equivalent to `Curve::read_G`) for `EncryptionKeyMessage.enc_key` and `EncryptedMessage.key` in `crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs` (lines 58 and 173), or explicitly check `enc_key.is_identity()` / `msg.key.is_identity()` during registration and decryption and return an error. Optionally also require a proof of possession for the registered `enc_key` so a participant cannot register a key they cannot decrypt for (e.g. identity or another participant's key), closing the related co-option variant where shares are encrypted to a key the claimed recipient does not control.

### Proof of Concept

1. Participant `M` runs the DKG commitment round and serializes an `EncryptionKeyMessage` whose trailing `enc_key` field is the 32-byte canonical identity encoding (all-zero `CompressedEdwardsY` for dalek suites). `EncryptionKeyMessage::read` accepts it because `Point::from_bytes` succeeds and `Ciphersuite::read_G` performs no identity check (`crypto/ciphersuite/src/lib.rs:91-101`, `crypto/dalek-ff-group/src/lib.rs:429-437`).
2. Each honest participant `i` calls `Encryption::encrypt(rng, context, M, share)`, which computes `ecdh(&k_i, identity) = identity` and encrypts the share under `cipher(context, identity)` — a keystream any third party can regenerate (`crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs:101-133, 153-154`).
3. A passive observer collecting the `n` `EncryptedMessage`s addressed to `M` applies the same keystream and recovers every `f_i(M)`, hence `M`'s threshold share, while `calculate_share`/`batch.verify_with_vartime_blame` all succeed normally — no error, no blame, no indication the encryption was vacuous.

```markdown
### Blame

Since each message has a distinct key attached, and accordingly a distinct
shared key, it's possible to reveal the shared key for a specific message
without revealing any other message's decryption keys. This is utilized when a
participant misbehaves. A participant who receives an invalid encrypted message
publishes its key, able to without concern for side effects, With the key
published, all participants can decrypt the message in order to decide blame.

While key reuse by a participant is considered as them revealing the messages
themselves, and therefore out of scope, there is an attack where a malicious
adversary claims another participant's encryption key. They'll fail to encrypt
their message, and the recipient will issue a blame statement. This blame
statement, intended to reveal the malicious adversary, also reveals the message
by the participant whose keys were co-opted. To resolve this, a
proof-of-possession is also included with encrypted messages, ensuring only
those actually with per-message keys can claim to use them.
```
