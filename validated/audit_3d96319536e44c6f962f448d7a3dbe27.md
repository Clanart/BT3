### Title
PedPoP encryption-key registration lacks proof of possession, letting a participant claim another's key and leak their secret share - (File: crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs)

### Summary
The incident class is information leakage: data entrusted to a third party is disclosed to an unauthorized party. The analog in Serai is in PedPoP's DKG encryption layer. Each participant broadcasts an `EncryptionKeyMessage` containing a long-term encryption public key (`enc_key`), and senders encrypt each recipient's FROST secret share via ECDH against that key (`encrypt()` → `ecdh(key, to)` → `cipher(...)`) [1](#0-0) [2](#0-1) . Registration (`Decryption::register`) stores `msg.enc_key` with no proof of possession of the corresponding private key [3](#0-2) .

The per-message PoP (`pop_challenge`) was added precisely to stop a leaked-message-via-blame variant of this attack, but it only binds `context`, `nonce`, `key`, `sender`, and `msg` — it protects the per-message key, not the registration-time `enc_key` [4](#0-3) . Nothing binds `enc_key` to its owner, so an attacker can register a victim's public encryption key as their own.

### Impact Explanation
If participant Eve registers Bob's `enc_pub_key` (which is public, broadcast in round 1), every other participant encrypts Eve's Pedersen share `Σ poly_j(Eve)` under Bob's key [5](#0-4) . Bob — holding the private key — runs `Encryption::decrypt`, which computes `ecdh(self.enc_key, msg.key)` and decrypts successfully [6](#0-5) . Bob thereby recovers a secret share that was addressed to Eve, i.e., key-share material is disclosed to a party it was never intended for. This reduces the effective security margin of the threshold: Bob now legitimately holds shares at two participant indexes, and the protocol's non-equivocation assumption (each participant holds exactly one share) is silently violated. Meanwhile Eve still passes share verification because the share itself is valid — only the confidentiality boundary was broken, so the leak is undetectable.

### Likelihood Explanation
The attack requires only a malicious DKG participant sending crafted public round-1 bytes (`EncryptionKeyMessage` with a copied `enc_key`) — fully within the unprivileged-public-input threat model, and the spec itself acknowledges key-copying as a live concern that required the per-message PoP mitigation [7](#0-6) . It needs the victim (Bob) to later use his decryption key normally, which is the honest protocol path. The gain is one extra share rather than full key recovery, keeping severity at Medium.

### Recommendation
Bind `enc_key` to its owner at registration: require a Schnorr proof of possession over `enc_key` (transcript including `context` and the participant index), verified in `SecretShareMachine::verify_r1` alongside the existing commitment PoK batch [8](#0-7) , and reject duplicate `enc_key` values across distinct participants in `Decryption::register` [3](#0-2) .

### Proof of Concept
1. Run an n-of-n PedPoP session. In round 1, honest Bob publishes `EncryptionKeyMessage { enc_key: B_pub, msg: commitments_b }`.
2. Malicious Eve publishes `EncryptionKeyMessage { enc_key: B_pub, msg: commitments_e }` — same `B_pub`, her own commitments. `verify_r1` accepts it; only `msg.sig` (over `commitments[0]`) is checked, never `enc_key` [9](#0-8) .
3. In round 2 every participant calls `self.encryption.encrypt(rng, Eve, share_e)`, which ECDHs against `decryption.enc_keys[&Eve] = B_pub` [5](#0-4) .
4. Bob calls `Encryption::decrypt` on the message addressed to Eve: `ecdh(b_priv, msg.key)` reproduces the cipher key, revealing `share_e` in clear [10](#0-9) .
5. `share_verification_statements` still passes for Eve's index, so `calculate_share` returns success — the leak is silent [11](#0-10) .

**File:** spec/cryptography/Distributed Key Generation.md (L28-35)
```markdown
While key reuse by a participant is considered as them revealing the messages
themselves, and therefore out of scope, there is an attack where a malicious
adversary claims another participant's encryption key. They'll fail to encrypt
their message, and the recipient will issue a blame statement. This blame
statement, intended to reveal the malicious adversary, also reveals the message
by the participant whose keys were co-opted. To resolve this, a
proof-of-possession is also included with encrypted messages, ensuring only
those actually with per-message keys can claim to use them.
```
