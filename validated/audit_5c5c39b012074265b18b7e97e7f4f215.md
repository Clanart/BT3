### Title
DKG encryption-key registration accepts unauthenticated `enc_key` (no PoP / no binding to participant identity) enabling share theft by an in-flight substitution - (File: crypto/dkg/pedpop/src/encryption.rs)

### Summary
`EncryptionKeyMessage` attaches an `enc_key` to each participant's `Commitments` broadcast, but the PedPoP proof-of-knowledge only proves knowledge of the coefficient behind `commitments[0]` — it never binds `enc_key` to the sender or proves possession of its discrete log. `Decryption::register` inserts the attacker-controlled point directly into `enc_keys` under the message's participant index. A party able to rewrite registration traffic (the same interception model as the referenced advisory, and explicitly not ruled out by the "authenticated channel" assumption — a malicious/misdirected relay or a participant that forwards re-keyed copies) can substitute `enc_key` while leaving the still-valid `Commitments` untouched. Every sender then encrypts victim `l`'s secret shares to the attacker's key in `Encryption::encrypt` (`self.decryption.enc_keys[&participant]`), and the attacker recovers `l`'s final secret share after decrypting all `t`-share contributions — without `l` or anyone else observing a failure if the attacker re-encrypts and relays onward to `l`'s real key.

### Finding Description
- `EncryptionKeyMessage` is `{ msg: M, enc_key: C::G }`; `enc_key` is read with `C::read_G` and written verbatim — no signature, no PoK, no inclusion in any transcript. [1](#0-0) 
- `Decryption::register` stores `msg.enc_key` keyed by the caller-supplied `Participant` index; the only check is a duplicate-registration assert. [2](#0-1) 
- The PedPoP PoK challenge binds `context`, the participant index `l`, the nonce, and the serialized commitments — but `cached_msg` covers only the commitment points, never `enc_key`. So swapping `enc_key` keeps `msg.sig` valid. [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 
- `verify_r1` registers the (possibly substituted) `enc_key` before batch-verifying the PoK, which still passes. [5](#0-4) 
- Shares for participant `l` are then encrypted via `encrypt(rng, l, share)` → `ecdh(key, enc_keys[&l])` → ChaCha20 keystream, so the attacker holding `log(enc_keys[l])` decrypts every share addressed to `l`. [6](#0-5) [7](#0-6) 
- The in-code comment admits the channel is only "assumed-to-exist" external authentication and that per-message keys have "no root of trust" — yet `enc_key` itself, the root key for all share confidentiality, carries no proof at all, while the per-message `pop` exists precisely to prevent key-substitution side effects. The same substitution concern was engineered away for per-message keys but not for the registered encryption key. [8](#0-7) [9](#0-8) 

### Impact Explanation
Key share recovery. An attacker who substitutes `enc_key` in participant `l`'s registration message receives every `EncryptedMessage<C, SecretShare>` that honest participants compute for `l` (`generate_secret_shares`, `lib.rs:366-369`). Each share is a field element; the attacker decrypts them via the same `ecdh`/`cipher` path and sums them to obtain `l`'s complete secret share for the resulting `ThresholdKeys`. If the attacker also controls a share (e.g., is a participant in a 2-of-2, or t-1 corrupted participants plus one stolen share), the full group private key is recoverable, allowing unilateral signing of arbitrary Bitcoin transactions/messages under the multisig key. The victim still completes `calculate_share` normally (the attacker re-encrypts each share to `l`'s real key and relays it with a fresh valid `pop`, which it can produce since it controls the per-message key), so no blame is raised and the compromise is silent.

### Likelihood Explanation
Requires tampering with registration traffic between participants — precisely the "registration could be intercepted or misdirected" condition of the advisory, and reachable entirely through untrusted bytes fed to `EncryptionKeyMessage::read`/`Commitments::read`. The crate itself acknowledges the channel assumption is external ("doesn't mean the DKG offers an authenticated channel"), so deployments relying on relayed or coordinator-forwarded commitment messages (e.g., `processor/src/key_gen.rs` broadcasts serialized commitments through the coordinator) expose exactly this surface. No collusion threshold is needed; a single network position suffices.

### Recommendation
Bind `enc_key` to the sender inside the round-1 message: extend `challenge` (or add a second Schnorr PoK) so the signature proves possession of `enc_key`'s discrete log and commits to `enc_key`, the participant index, and `context`. Concretely, include `enc_key.to_bytes()` in the `challenge` transcript fed to `Commitments.sig` and add a `SchnorrSignature` over the `enc_key` scalar verified in `verify_r1`/`Decryption::register` before inserting into `enc_keys`. Reject registrations lacking a valid PoP rather than asserting only on duplicates.

### Proof of Concept
1. Victim `l` emits `EncryptionKeyMessage { msg: Commitments{commitments, cached_msg, sig}, enc_key: K_l }`.
2. Attacker intercepts it, replaces `enc_key` with `K_a = g * a` (own key), keeps `msg` (including `sig`) byte-identical, and forwards under index `l`. `challenge(context, l, sig.R, cached_msg)` is unchanged → `verify_r1` batch verification passes.
3. Every honest sender `s` calls `encryption.encrypt(rng, l, share_s(l))`, computing `ecdh(k_s, K_a)` — decryptable by the attacker via `a * msg.key`.
4. Attacker decrypts all `n-1` shares for `l`, re-encrypts each under `K_l` with its own fresh `key`/`pop`, and forwards to `l`. `l` completes normally.
5. Attacker computes `secret_share_l = Σ_s share_s(l)` → `ThresholdKeys` for `l` fully compromised; combined with any other share set reaching threshold, the group key is recovered.
