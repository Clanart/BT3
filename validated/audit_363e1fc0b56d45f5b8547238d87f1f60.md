### Title
Unprivileged spam-purchases can evict a victim app's paid bandwidth subscriptions before consumption - (File: `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs`)

### Summary
`pallet-bandwidth`'s `push_subscription` enforces a hard 1024-entry FIFO cap per `(app_chain, app)` and silently evicts the *oldest* subscription whenever a new one is pushed onto a full list [1](#0-0) . Because `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on the EVM side lets **any caller** pay for bandwidth credited to an **arbitrary `app` byte string**, with no ownership check tying the caller to the `app` [2](#0-1) , and `on_accept` only verifies the message came from the registered manager contract — not that the purchaser is authorized to fund that specific `app` [3](#0-2)  — an attacker can flood a target `(app_chain, app)` bucket with 1024 minimal purchases to forcibly evict the victim's already-paid, unconsumed subscription entries, destroying paid-for bandwidth capacity.

### Finding Description
The core broken invariant mirrors the TPDA report: a capacity/limit mechanism (`type(uint96).max` mint limit in the original; `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024` FIFO cap here) that is meant to bound normal usage can instead be *reached deliberately by an unprivileged third party* to damage another party's legitimate, already-paid-for entitlement.

- `push_subscription` unconditionally evicts index `0` (the oldest entry) once the list length equals `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`, regardless of whether that entry belongs to the "attacker" or a legitimate payer, and regardless of how much of its `remaining_bytes` is unused [4](#0-3) .
- The list is keyed purely by `(app_chain, app)`, not by payer [5](#0-4) , so anyone who can produce a valid purchase message targeting that `(chain, app)` pair contributes to (and can overflow) the same bounded list.
- `BandwidthManager.purchase(bytes calldata app, uint256 tier, uint256 months, bytes calldata chain)` is a fully public function; it validates only that the tier is priced and pulls payment from `msg.sender` — it never checks that `msg.sender` is the owner/operator of `app` [2](#0-1) . Sponsorship is an explicit, documented feature ("a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base") [6](#0-5) , confirming any address can fund/target any `(chain, app)` bucket.
- On the pallet side, `on_accept` only checks `request.from == registered manager` [7](#0-6) ; it performs no per-purchaser authorization for the `app` field decoded from `msg.app`, and directly calls `push_subscription` with attacker-controlled `chain`/`app`/`tier`/`months` [8](#0-7) .

The documentation itself only reasons about *accidental* overflow ("at the default of one purchase per cycle, 1024 buys is years of headroom") [9](#0-8) , and never considers an adversary deliberately racing 1024 cheap purchases against a target app to evict its real, high-value subscription before the gate ever drains it. This is exactly the same broken-invariant shape as the source report: a legitimate consumer's throughput is destroyed by an attacker manufacturing enough capacity-consuming events to hit a hard limit, at the cost of buying (cheap) capacity themselves.

### Impact Explanation
This falls under "loss of funds" and "transaction manipulation" in the impact gate: a paying app can have its already-settled, unconsumed bandwidth entitlement destroyed by an unrelated, unprivileged third party without needing a compromised relayer, prover, or admin. The victim app paid real fee-token value for a `Subscription` whose `remaining_bytes` may be almost entirely untouched; eviction (`Event::SubscriptionEvicted`, explicitly documented as "what the user paid for and won't get to use" [10](#0-9) ) permanently destroys that value with no refund path in the gate logic itself. Beyond fund loss, this also causes denial of the app's cross-chain messaging capability once its bandwidth is wiped, since the gate rejects any further requests once the ledger is empty [11](#0-10) .

### Likelihood Explanation
This requires no privileged role, no relayer/prover collusion, and no front-running of any transaction — the attacker only needs to submit 1024 (or fewer, if the bucket is already partially full) `purchase()` calls against the cheapest configured tier, targeting the victim's `(chain, app)` pair, before the victim's subscription is drained by the gate. The attack cost scales with the cheapest tier price × months × number of evictions needed, which is comparable in nature to the "requires large capital" caveat that kept the original TPDA finding at Medium severity rather than High — this is consistent with a Medium-severity bounty submission here as well. It is entirely deterministic (no market timing, no oracle manipulation) and repeatable against any `(chain, app)` pair the attacker chooses.

### Recommendation
- Do not evict a subscription with non-trivial `remaining_bytes` purely because the list is full; instead reject new pushes once the list is full and the caller isn't the legitimate app/payer, or expand the cap dynamically.
- Track subscriptions per payer (or require `msg.sender == app` / an authorized relationship) so an unrelated address cannot inflate another app's FIFO list.
- Alternatively, weight eviction by remaining value (evict only fully-or-mostly-drained entries) rather than strict insertion order, or refund fee-token value proportional to `lost_bytes` when `SubscriptionEvicted` fires.

### Proof of Concept
1. Victim app `A` purchases a `TierFour` (largest, most expensive) subscription for `(chain=EVM-8453, app=A)`, credited via `BandwidthManager.purchase()` → `pallet-bandwidth::on_accept` → `push_subscription` appends entry #1 to `Allowance[(EVM-8453, A)]`.
2. Attacker calls `BandwidthManager.purchase(app=A, tier=TierOne (cheapest), months=1, chain="EVM-8453")` 1023 times in quick succession (any `msg.sender`, no relationship to `A` required, per `purchase()`'s lack of an app-ownership check) [2](#0-1) .
3. Each purchase dispatches an ISMP POST to `pallet-bandwidth`; each `on_accept` call passes the manager check and calls `push_subscription(app_chain=EVM-8453, app=A, TierOne, bytes, duration)` [8](#0-7) .
4. Once `Allowance[(EVM-8453, A)]` reaches 1024 entries, each further attacker purchase evicts the oldest entry via `list.remove(0)` [4](#0-3) . Since victim `A`'s `TierFour` purchase was inserted first (oldest, lowest `purchased_at`), it is evicted before its `remaining_bytes` are consumed, emitting `SubscriptionEvicted { lost_bytes: <A's paid bytes> }`.
5. App `A` has lost the fee-token value of its `TierFour` purchase and, if no other subscriptions remain, is now blocked by the gate (`GateError::NoAllowance`) until it repurchases — an unprivileged attacker directly caused loss of funds and denial of service to a legitimate paying app.

## The Gate

Every non-purchase POST request from a registered source chain runs through `BandwidthGate::try_consume(source, app, bytes)`:

1. If the app is on the **allowlist** for that source, return `Ok` without touching the ledger.
2. Sweep expired subscriptions in place.
3. If no live subscriptions remain → `GateError::NoAllowance`.
4. Sum `remaining_bytes` across live entries. If the sum is short → `GateError::Insufficient { remaining, required }`. **No mutation happens in this case** — the caller can retry after a top-up.
5. Otherwise drain from the head until the requested bytes are satisfied. Pop entries that hit zero. Emit `BandwidthConsumed` with the post-deduct remaining.

The "no mutation on insufficient" property is load-bearing: it means a top-up race is safe — if a message arrives between when an app notices it's short and when the top-up lands, the message stays rejectable rather than half-consuming the subscription.
```

## Sponsorship

The purchase message carries its own `chain` (the _credit chain_) which is **independent of the source chain** that sent the message. This means a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base by dispatching a purchase whose payload sets `chain = "EVM-8453"`.

The pallet keys allowance storage by `(app_chain, app)` taken from the message body, not by `request.source`. The event `BandwidthCredited` carries both — `app_chain` (where the credit lands) and `paid_from` (where the payment came from) — so the cross-chain payer is auditable.

This is what makes the system multi-tenant friendly: a treasury on a single chain can sponsor bandwidth for an app deployed across many chains, without having to deploy `BandwidthManager` on each chain the app lives on.
```
