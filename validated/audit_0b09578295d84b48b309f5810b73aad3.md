## Analysis

The external VUSD.sol bug is: an unprivileged actor calls a cheap, minimum-gated public entrypoint repeatedly to fill a bounded FIFO queue, delaying/harming genuine users whose entries get stuck behind the spam. The local analog in Hyperbridge is in the **bandwidth prepayment system**: `BandwidthManager.purchase()` (EVM, public, unprivileged) → `pallet-bandwidth::on_accept` → `push_subscription`, which appends to a `BoundedVec<Subscription, MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS(1024)>` FIFO keyed by `(app_chain, app)` and **evicts the oldest entry outright** when the list is full [1](#0-0) .

Critically, the `app` and `chain` fields of a purchase are caller-supplied and are never checked against `msg.sender`/payer identity — this is the intended "sponsorship" feature [2](#0-1) [3](#0-2) . So any address can target any victim `(chain, app)` bucket with cheap purchases, and the eviction logic is pure FIFO regardless of value: [4](#0-3) 

The lost bytes of the evicted subscription are gone forever — the event comment says it plainly: `lost_bytes` is "what the user paid for and won't get to use" [5](#0-4) . There is no floor that ties eviction cost to the value destroyed: an attacker pays the cheapest configured tier price (e.g. $50/month tier) while potentially destroying an untouched, unexpired top-tier subscription worth up to $1000 for 8MB [6](#0-5) .

### Title
Unprivileged, cheap `purchase()` calls can force-evict another app's unused prepaid bandwidth subscription via the 1024-cap FIFO eviction in `pallet-bandwidth` - (File: `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs`, `evm/src/apps/BandwidthManager.sol`)

### Summary
`BandwidthManager.purchase()` lets anyone credit any `(chain, app)` bucket by choosing arbitrary `app`/`chain` bytes, with no relation to the caller. On the Hyperbridge side, `pallet-bandwidth::push_subscription` stores each `(chain, app)`'s subscriptions in a `BoundedVec` capped at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024`; once full, every new purchase evicts index 0 (the oldest entry) unconditionally, destroying its unused `remaining_bytes`. Because the eviction is strict FIFO with no value-weighting and purchases into a victim's bucket require no authorization, a low-cost attacker can grow or top off a target app's queue with the cheapest tier and force-evict a legitimate, unconsumed, higher-value subscription that the victim (or its sponsor) paid for.

### Finding Description
- `purchase()` on `BandwidthManager.sol` takes `app` and `chain` as raw caller-supplied parameters with zero binding to `msg.sender`; this is documented as the intentional "sponsorship" model [7](#0-6) .
- The pallet's `on_accept` decodes the purchase and calls `push_subscription(&msg.chain, &key, ...)`, where `msg.chain`/`msg.app` come straight from the wire message, not from anything tied to who paid [8](#0-7) .
- `push_subscription` unconditionally evicts `list[0]` (`list.remove(0)`) whenever the bounded list is already at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`, regardless of the evicted entry's remaining bytes, tier, or how recently it was purchased/consumed [4](#0-3) .
- The gate (`try_consume`) also drains strictly FIFO from the head [9](#0-8) , meaning the entry most likely to be evicted next is also the one closest to being legitimately consumed — i.e. an attacker's forced eviction directly substitutes for organic consumption, destroying value that would otherwise have gone to the app.
- Nothing enforces that the entry being evicted has to belong to the attacker, nor that eviction cost is proportional to destroyed value: the attacker pays whatever the cheapest configured tier costs (`tierPrice[tier]` on the manager, floor set only by governance and by `PriceNotRepresentable`/`UnknownTier` checks, not by value-parity with victim holdings) [2](#0-1) .

This directly mirrors the VUSD analog: an unprivileged actor spams a cheap, minimally-gated public call to abuse a hard-capped FIFO structure and destroy/deny value that rightfully belongs to another party — except here the outcome isn't merely delay, it is permanent, irreversible loss of prepaid "bandwidth balance," which the task's pivots explicitly call out as an asset that "must move exactly once and only to the rightful beneficiary and amount."

### Impact Explanation
An attacker can permanently destroy another app's prepaid, unconsumed bandwidth allowance for a fraction of what the victim paid, by targeting the victim's `(chain, app)` FIFO with cheap tier purchases until eviction triggers. This is loss of funds/value for the rightful beneficiary (the victim app or its sponsor) caused entirely through an unauthenticated, unprivileged public entrypoint — no relayer, prover, or admin compromise required. If the victim's bucket is already near capacity (encouraged by the multi-sponsor design), only a handful of cheap purchases are needed to evict a large, expensive, still-unused subscription.

### Likelihood Explanation
High for any actively-used, multi-sponsored `(chain, app)` bucket approaching the 1024 cap, since the attacker only needs enough cheap purchases to close the remaining gap and then one more to trigger eviction of the target's subscription. Even from an empty queue, the attack is fully deterministic and requires no privileged access or timing races beyond simple transaction ordering — cost scales linearly with how far the queue is from capacity, and the cheapest tier sets the attacker's cost floor regardless of what is being destroyed.

### Recommendation
- Do not evict strictly by insertion order irrespective of value; e.g., weight eviction by remaining value, or refuse to evict subscriptions that still have unused bytes when non-full/lower-value slots are available.
- Tie `purchase()`'s target `app` to the payer/caller by default, and require an explicit allowlist/authorization step (already present for the `Allowlist` governance flag) before a third party can credit — i.e. sponsorship should be opt-in from the recipient's side, or capped in how much a non-owner can top up.
- Alternatively, key subscriptions per-payer within the `(chain, app)` bucket so one sponsor's cheap purchases can only evict that same sponsor's own oldest entries, never another payer's.
- Consider raising `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` or moving to per-payer sub-accounting to remove the shared-queue griefing surface entirely.

### Proof of Concept
1. Victim app `A` on `chain = X` has an active, largely-unconsumed Tier4 subscription (e.g. 8MB / ~$1000) sitting at some position in `Allowance::<T>::get(X, A)`.
2. Attacker repeatedly calls `BandwidthManager.purchase(app = A, tier = 1 (cheapest), months = 1, chain = X)` on the EVM source chain registered for `X` [10](#0-9) .
3. Each purchase dispatches a `BandwidthPurchaseMsg` to `pallet-bandwidth`, which calls `push_subscription(&X, &A, ...)` [3](#0-2) .
4. Once `Allowance::<T>::get(X, A)` reaches 1024 entries (either pre-existing from organic sponsorship activity, or built up by the attacker's own cheap purchases), each subsequent attacker purchase evicts `list[0]` via `list.remove(0)` [4](#0-3) .
5. When the victim's Tier4 subscription reaches the head of the FIFO (via normal drain/expiry of older entries), the attacker's next cheap purchase evicts it, emitting `SubscriptionEvicted { lost_bytes: <victim's unused bytes> }` — permanently destroying value the victim paid for, at a fraction of the cost.

		/// is already at `MaxSubscriptions`, evict the oldest entry and
		/// emit [`Event::SubscriptionEvicted`] so the lost bytes are
		/// auditable. Returns the new subscription's `expires_at`.
		fn push_subscription(
			app_chain: &StateMachine,
			app: &AppKey,
			tier: TierIndex,
			bytes: BandwidthBytes,
			duration_secs: u64,
		) -> u64 {
			let now = <T as pallet_ismp::Config>::TimestampProvider::now().as_secs();
			let expires_at = now.saturating_add(duration_secs);
			let new_sub =
				Subscription { tier, remaining_bytes: bytes, expires_at, purchased_at: now };

			let evicted = Allowance::<T>::mutate(app_chain, app, |list| {
				let evicted = if list.len() == MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS as usize {
					Some(list.remove(0))
				} else {
					None
				};
				// Capacity is now guaranteed; try_push can't fail.
				let _ = list.try_push(new_sub);
				evicted
			});

    function purchase(bytes calldata app, uint256 tier, uint256 months, bytes calldata chain)
        external
        returns (bytes32 commitment)
    {
        if (app.length == 0 || chain.length == 0 || months == 0) revert InvalidPurchase();
        uint256 price18d = tierPrice[tier];
        if (price18d == 0) revert UnknownTier();

        uint256 total18d = price18d * months;
        address feeToken = IDispatcher(_host).feeToken();
        uint8 dec = IERC20Metadata(feeToken).decimals();
        uint256 scale = 10 ** (18 - dec);
        if (total18d % scale != 0) revert PriceNotRepresentable();
        uint256 amount = total18d / scale;

        IERC20(feeToken).safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);

        BandwidthPurchaseMsg memory body = BandwidthPurchaseMsg({
            app: app,
            tier: tier,
            months: months,
            chain: chain
        });

        commitment = IDispatcher(_host).dispatch(
            DispatchPost({
                dest: IDispatcher(_host).hyperbridge(),
                to: PALLET_BANDWIDTH_MODULE_ID,
                body: abi.encode(body),
                timeout: 0,
                fee: 0,
                payer: address(this)
            })

## Sponsorship

The purchase message carries its own `chain` (the _credit chain_) which is **independent of the source chain** that sent the message. This means a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base by dispatching a purchase whose payload sets `chain = "EVM-8453"`.

The pallet keys allowance storage by `(app_chain, app)` taken from the message body, not by `request.source`. The event `BandwidthCredited` carries both — `app_chain` (where the credit lands) and `paid_from` (where the payment came from) — so the cross-chain payer is auditable.

This is what makes the system multi-tenant friendly: a treasury on a single chain can sponsor bandwidth for an app deployed across many chains, without having to deploy `BandwidthManager` on each chain the app lives on.
