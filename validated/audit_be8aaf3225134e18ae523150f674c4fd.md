### Title
Permissionless Cross-Chain Bandwidth Sponsorship Allows Griefing Eviction of Any App's Paid Subscription - ([File: modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs])

### Summary
`pallet_bandwidth::Pallet::on_accept` credits a bandwidth subscription to an `(app_chain, app)` key taken entirely from the untrusted purchase message body (`msg.chain`, `msg.app`), not from `request.source`/`request.from` beyond manager authentication. Combined with a hard-capped, FIFO-with-oldest-eviction `SubscriptionList` (`MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024`), any account on any registered source chain can pay the cheapest configured tier repeatedly and target an arbitrary victim app on an arbitrary chain, forcing eviction of that victim's legitimately-purchased, unconsumed bandwidth. This is the same broken invariant as the seed report: unrestricted, low-cost creation of accounting entries with no per-target rate limit or authorization, enabling spam that destroys another party's already-paid-for resource.

### Finding Description
`push_subscription` (`modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:404-437`) appends every new subscription to `Allowance<T>` keyed by `(app_chain, app)`. When the list is already at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` (1024), it unconditionally evicts index 0 — the oldest live subscription, regardless of its remaining bytes or expiry — and emits `SubscriptionEvicted` acknowledging "lost bytes" as an expected but unaudited-for-abuse outcome: [1](#0-0) 

`on_accept` derives the credited key straight from the purchaser-controlled message body (`msg.chain`, `msg.app`) rather than binding it to the caller's own identity: [2](#0-1) 

The only check on the purchase path is that `request.from` matches the registered `BandwidthManager` for the *source chain* — there is no check tying `msg.app` to the caller, no per-`(app_chain, app)` purchase rate limit, and no minimum-remaining-bytes protection before eviction: [3](#0-2) 

This "sponsorship" design is explicitly documented as intentional (`chain` "not validated against the source chain"), but the security consequence — that *anyone* can direct purchases at *any* app's ledger — is not offset by any anti-griefing control. An attacker who buys the cheapest tier (`TierOne`) 1024 times can fully evict a victim's large, expensive, still-live subscription (e.g. a `TierFour` purchase paid for 8MB/month) before it is consumed, at a cost potentially much lower than the victim's original purchase, since eviction is strictly FIFO-by-insertion-order and ignores subscription size or value.

### Impact Explanation
This falls squarely under "bridged assets ... bandwidth balances must move exactly once and only to the rightful beneficiary and amount." A victim app that pre-paid for bandwidth can have that paid-for, unconsumed allowance destroyed by an unrelated third party without the victim's consent, at asymmetric cost to the attacker (cheap tier vs. expensive tier), resulting in real fund loss (the victim paid fee tokens for bytes it can never use) and denial of service (the victim's app subsequently gets ISMP messages rejected by the `BandwidthGate` for lack of allowance) even though it paid in good faith.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account able to call `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on a registered source chain (i.e., any unprivileged EVM user with funds to buy the cheapest tier) can execute this — no relayer, prover, admin, or governance actor is required. The `app` and `chain` fields of the purchase message are fully attacker-controlled and are the only inputs that decide which victim's ledger is attacked, and the FIFO/1024-cap eviction logic performs no sanity check tying eviction size/value to the incoming purchase. The main constraint is the cost of buying 1024 cheapest-tier subscriptions, which is a bounded, attacker-known, and generally modest amount relative to draining a high-value target's balance.

### Recommendation
- Bind credited subscriptions to the purchasing identity rather than allowing an arbitrary `(chain, app)` pair to be targeted by any sender, or require an explicit authorization/allowlist from the target app before third-party sponsorship is accepted.
- Replace strict FIFO-oldest eviction with a policy that protects subscriptions above some remaining-value threshold, or track and refund/queue overflow rather than silently destroying paid-for allowance.
- Add a per-`(app_chain, app)` purchase rate limit or a minimum tier-value floor for cross-chain sponsorship, and consider making the 1024 cap per-payer or per-tier rather than a single shared queue, so a single cheap-tier attacker cannot cheaply exhaust or evict a victim's expensive, high-value subscriptions.

### Proof of Concept
1. Governance registers `BandwidthManager` for chain `EVM-X` and configures `TierOne` (cheap) and `TierFour` (expensive, large byte budget) tiers.
2. Victim buys `TierFour` for app `V` on `app_chain = EVM-Y`, crediting a subscription with a large `remaining_bytes` via `BandwidthManager.purchase()` → `on_accept` → `push_subscription` (`modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:467-478`).
3. Attacker, from `EVM-X` (or any registered source chain), calls `purchase()` 1024 times with `tier = TierOne`, `app = V`, `chain = EVM-Y` — the same `(app_chain, app)` key as the victim, exploiting the fact that `chain`/`app` are unrestricted purchaser-controlled fields (`docs/content/developers/evm/bandwidth/purchasing.mdx:192-205`, `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:105-108`).
4. Each purchase invokes `push_subscription`, and once the list length hits `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`, each subsequent purchase evicts index 0 (`modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:416-425`) — after enough cheap purchases, the victim's `TierFour` entry (now at the front of the FIFO queue) is evicted and `SubscriptionEvicted` fires with the victim's `lost_bytes`.
5. The victim's app now has no allowance despite having paid for 8MB of bandwidth, and subsequent legitimate cross-chain dispatches from app `V` are rejected by `BandwidthGate::try_consume` (`modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:509-564`) until it re-purchases — funds lost, service denied.

Note: I could not fully verify the exact fee-token cost asymmetry (tier prices are governance-configured per deployment and not fixed in code), so the economic feasibility depends on the specific tier pricing chosen by each deployment's governance; the logic flaw (unauthenticated cross-app targeting + value-blind FIFO eviction) itself is confirmed directly in the pallet code.
