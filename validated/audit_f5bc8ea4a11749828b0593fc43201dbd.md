### Title
Unpermissioned `purchase()` lets any caller grief a victim's `(chain, app)` bandwidth allowance via FIFO eviction - ([File: modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs], [File: evm/src/apps/BandwidthManager.sol])

### Summary
`pallet-bandwidth`'s subscription ledger is a per-`(app_chain, app)` FIFO list capped at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` (1024). `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on the EVM side accepts an arbitrary `app` (and `chain`) parameter from *any* caller — this is intentional "sponsorship," documented as letting a treasury top up bandwidth for apps it doesn't own. But because the pallet's eviction policy on a full list always evicts the *oldest* entry regardless of remaining value, any unauthenticated party can flood a victim `(app_chain, app)` bucket with minimal, cheap purchases to push out (evict) the victim's legitimately-paid, high-value, unexpired subscription — destroying bandwidth the victim already paid for. This mirrors the external report's core pattern: a public/unauthenticated entrypoint is allowed to consume/corrupt a shared, capacity-limited resource that gates a legitimate party's access, without any authorization check tying the caller to the resource being affected.

### Finding Description
`BandwidthManager.purchase()` is a fully public function; the `app` field is attacker-controlled calldata, not derived from `msg.sender`: [1](#0-0) 

The pallet credits the subscription onto the `(app_chain, app)` list keyed purely by the message body's `app`/`chain` fields — not by who paid: [2](#0-1) 

The FIFO list is `BoundedVec<Subscription, MaxSubscriptions>` capped at 1024, and documentation confirms pushing onto a full list evicts the oldest entry — irrespective of its remaining, still-valid byte balance: [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 

The `Subscription` struct and its `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`/eviction semantics: [5](#0-4) 

An attacker who wants to grief a specific victim `app` on a specific `app_chain` simply calls `purchase()` 1024 times (or enough times to overtake the victim's remaining slots) with the cheapest tier and `months=1`, targeting the victim's `(app_chain, app)` key. Each call is a legitimate on-chain purchase — no `onlyOwner`/allowlist check restricts who may credit whose bucket — so the pallet's `on_accept` will happily append every one of these garbage subscriptions to the victim's list. Once the list hits the 1024 cap, further pushes evict the oldest entries first, which — because the attacker's purchases post-date the victim's real (larger) purchase — means the victim's real, still-live, high-`remaining_bytes` subscription is evicted and its value is lost, even though it hadn't expired and had not been drained by any real usage.

This is the same underlying flaw class as the external report: a shared/limited resource (there: the global unauth rate-limit bucket; here: the finite 1024-slot FIFO per `(chain, app)`) can be consumed/exhausted by requests from a party that has no legitimate claim on that specific resource slot, and doing so directly harms the legitimate resource owner — with no gate checking that the "credit" being pushed belongs to (or was requested/wanted by) the app being credited.

### Impact Explanation
This is a genuine fund-loss vector: the victim app paid real fee-token value (via `BandwidthManager.purchase()`, pulling ERC20 from the payer) for a subscription with a specific byte allowance and expiry. An attacker, at comparatively low cost (cheapest tier × many small purchases), can force the eviction of that paid-for, unexpired, high-value subscription before it is consumed — permanently destroying bandwidth the victim already paid for and had a right to use. This satisfies the "bridged assets / bandwidth balances must move exactly once and only to the rightful beneficiary and amount" pivot: the victim's paid allowance is lost/burned instead of being consumed by the victim's own traffic. It also functions as a denial-of-service: since bandwidth gates all non-purchase POST/GET traffic through `BandwidthGate::try_consume`, evicting a victim's allowance can render their app unable to dispatch ISMP messages until it repurchases, at the attacker's discretion and at a fraction of the cost the victim originally paid.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is high for any actively-used app with a visible/known address: the `app` and `chain` parameters of `purchase()` are public calldata fields, not permissioned, so the attack requires only knowledge of the victim's `app` bytes and chain id, both of which are necessarily public (they must be known to legitimate sponsors and are discoverable from on-chain purchase/credit events, e.g. `BandwidthCredited`/`BandwidthPurchased`). No relayer, prover, governance, or admin compromise is required — a single unprivileged EOA with a modest amount of the fee token can execute the entire attack directly against `BandwidthManager.purchase()`.

### Recommendation
1. Do not let eviction be purely FIFO-by-insertion-time; evict based on economic value (e.g., pick the entry with least remaining value, or weight eviction against `remaining_bytes` / cost paid) so a flood of cheap purchases cannot displace a high-value subscription.
2. Alternatively/additionally, rate-limit or cap how many subscriptions a single non-owner sponsor can push into another app's bucket within a window, or require the credited `app` to opt in to sponsorship (e.g., via an allowlist of permitted sponsor chains/addresses) before third-party credits are accepted.
3. Consider raising `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` eviction to skip entries with meaningfully large remaining balances, or refuse the eviction (revert the purchase) if it would displace an unexpired subscription above a configurable byte threshold, forcing the attacker to actually out-fund the defender rather than out-count them.

### Proof of Concept
1. Victim (a legitimate app) has already purchased a large subscription for `(app_chain = "EVM-8453", app = VICTIM_APP)` via `BandwidthManager.purchase(VICTIM_APP, tier=4, months=12, chain)`, crediting a `Subscription` with large `remaining_bytes` and a far-future `expires_at`.
2. Attacker (any unprivileged EOA) repeatedly calls `BandwidthManager.purchase(VICTIM_APP, tier=1, months=1, chain)` — the cheapest tier — targeting the same `(app_chain, VICTIM_APP)` key, at least `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` (1024) times (relayed through the standard purchase→ISMP POST→pallet `on_accept` credit flow, per `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs`).
3. Once the victim's `(app_chain, VICTIM_APP)` `SubscriptionList` (a `BoundedVec` capped at 1024, `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs:71-74`) reaches capacity, each subsequent attacker purchase evicts the oldest entry, per the documented eviction rule (`docs/content/developers/evm/bandwidth/overview.mdx:75-77`).
4. Because the victim's real purchase predates all the attacker's spam purchases, it sits at (or near) the head of the FIFO and is evicted first — its remaining bytes are lost (`SubscriptionEvicted` event fires with the victim's `lost_bytes`), while the attacker's worthless, low-value entries remain in the queue.
5. The victim's app is now unable to dispatch ISMP messages via `BandwidthGate::try_consume` (insufficient/no allowance) despite having paid for substantial bandwidth that had not expired or been used.

```text
### Eviction

Pushing onto a full list (1024 entries) evicts the **oldest** entry and emits `SubscriptionEvicted` with the lost bytes so the loss is auditable on-chain. In practice this only happens under pathological repeat-buy behavior — at the default of one purchase per cycle, 1024 buys is years of headroom.
```

```text
## Sponsorship

The purchase message carries its own `chain` (the _credit chain_) which is **independent of the source chain** that sent the message. This means a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base by dispatching a purchase whose payload sets `chain = "EVM-8453"`.

The pallet keys allowance storage by `(app_chain, app)` taken from the message body, not by `request.source`. The event `BandwidthCredited` carries both — `app_chain` (where the credit lands) and `paid_from` (where the payment came from) — so the cross-chain payer is auditable.

This is what makes the system multi-tenant friendly: a treasury on a single chain can sponsor bandwidth for an app deployed across many chains, without having to deploy `BandwidthManager` on each chain the app lives on.
```

```rust
//! # pallet-bandwidth
//!
//! Prepaid `(chain, app)` byte balances credited by tier purchases
//! from `BandwidthManager.sol`. Each purchase carries its own
//! `app_chain`, so any deployment can sponsor any app on any chain.
//!
//! Each `(chain, app)` row holds a FIFO list of [`Subscription`]s
//! (`BoundedVec`, capped at 1024). Every purchase appends a new
//! subscription with a fixed `expires_at`; expiry never extends and
//! same-tier repurchases don't stack — they queue. The gate drains
//! the oldest live subscription first; once empty it moves to the
//! next. Subscriptions that aren't reached before their expiry are
//! swept silently — what you paid for is yours only until it expires.
//! Pushes onto a full list evict the oldest entry and emit
//! [`Event::SubscriptionEvicted`].
```

```rust
/// drains via the gate, `expires_at` is fixed at purchase time and
/// never extends. Repurchases append a new row instead of stacking.
#[derive(
	Encode, Decode, DecodeWithMemTracking, TypeInfo, MaxEncodedLen, Clone, PartialEq, Eq, Debug,
)]
pub struct Subscription {
	/// SKU this subscription was bought against; for analytics/events
	/// only — the gate doesn't look at it during drain.
	pub tier: TierIndex,
	/// Bytes left to spend. Decrements as the gate drains; the entry
	/// is popped once this hits zero.
	pub remaining_bytes: BandwidthBytes,
	/// Unix seconds. Gate sweeps entries where `expires_at <= now`.
	pub expires_at: u64,
	/// Unix seconds at insertion — fixes FIFO order under same-block buys.
	pub purchased_at: u64,
}
```
