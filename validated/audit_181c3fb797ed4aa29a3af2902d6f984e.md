Confirmed: `GenerateIsolatedAddress` is deterministic per `(channelID, sender)` pair, meaning the same isolated address is reused for every IBC transfer-with-callback that a given sender routes through a given destination channel. This turns the flawed approval pattern in `IBCReceivePacketCallback` into a critical, reachable analog of the reported bug class.

### Title
Residual ERC20 allowance on deterministic isolated IBC-callback address enables cross-packet fund theft - (File: x/ibc/callbacks/keeper/keeper.go)

### Summary
`IBCReceivePacketCallback` sets an ERC20 `approve` from the isolated receiver address to the destination contract for the full packet amount, then invokes the destination contract, and only checks that the receiver's *balance* is zero afterward — it never checks or resets the *allowance*. Because the isolated address is deterministically derived from `(destChannel, sender)` and therefore reused across every future ICS-20 packet from that same sender/channel pair, any leftover (or attacker-inflated) allowance persists and can be drained by the (or a compromised) destination contract during a later, unrelated packet.

### Finding Description
In `IBCReceivePacketCallback`, the flow is: [1](#0-0) , then the arbitrary destination contract is invoked with attacker-supplied calldata: [2](#0-1) , and the only post-condition validated is that the isolated receiver's token *balance* is zero: [3](#0-2) .

Nothing resets or verifies the ERC20 allowance granted to `contractAddr` back to zero. The comment even says the intent is that the contract "must implement ... `transferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount)` for the total amount," but there is no code enforcing that the allowance is fully consumed — only that the balance is zero. If a contract's `transferFrom` call transfers less than the approved amount (e.g., due to a fee-on-transfer/rebasing token, a bug, or malicious behavior), or if the destination contract simply skips calling `transferFrom` for part of the amount, a positive allowance remains attached to the isolated address.

The isolated address is generated deterministically: [4](#0-3) , keyed only on `channelID` and `sender` (the packet sender bech32/string, attacker-controlled at least in the sense that the "sender" is whatever originated the IBC transfer). This means the same EVM address is reused as the callback receiver for every subsequent ICS-20 transfer-with-callback that the same sender routes through the same destination channel. Any stray allowance left after one packet's callback execution stays valid and spendable by the approved contract against any future coins landing in that same isolated address, entirely independent of the current packet's amount/receiver checks.

### Impact Explanation
This maps to the "Critical permanent freezing, locking, theft, or unauthorized extraction of user funds ... or token-pair-backed balances" impact class. A destination contract (attacker-controlled, or a legitimate contract that behaves unexpectedly, e.g. via reentrancy, partial transfer, or a bug) that is approved once can silently retain spend rights over an isolated address that will receive tokens from future, unrelated IBC packets sent by the same sender/channel — allowing extraction of funds that a later, honest user/packet delivers to that address, without the balance-zero check (evaluated only for the *current* packet's execution) catching the leftover allowance.

### Likelihood Explanation
Medium-to-High: it requires (a) a destination contract that does not fully consume its approved allowance in a single call (easy to construct deliberately, or occurs naturally with fee-on-transfer tokens or partial-failure logic), and (b) the same sender/channel pair being reused for a later transfer — both of which are entirely within the control of an unprivileged IBC-transfer-with-callback initiator, since `sender` and destination `channel` are attacker-chosen packet fields.

### Recommendation
After the destination contract call completes, explicitly re-check the ERC20 `allowance(isolatedAddr, contractAddr)` and revoke it (`approve(contractAddr, 0)`) if nonzero, in addition to (not instead of) the existing balance check. This closes the reused-address/residual-allowance window, mirroring the "always zero the allowance after use" mitigation recommended for the original approval-race-condition bug pattern.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker controls (or influences) a destination contract `C` registered as the callback target on this chain.
2. Attacker sends an ICS-20 transfer with a `destination-callback` memo pointing to `C`, sender `S`, amount `A1`, over channel `Ch`. The chain computes isolated address `I = GenerateIsolatedAddress(Ch, S)` and calls `approve(C, A1)` from `I`.
3. `C`'s callback logic (or a bug/partial-fee token behavior) calls `transferFrom(I, C, A1 - ε)`, leaving `I`'s balance at `ε` such that... actually to pass the balance==0 check, `C` must fully drain the balance; but it can do so via `transfer` from a different path while leaving the ERC20 *allowance* entry `(I → C)` un-zeroed (allowance and balance are independent state; draining balance to 0 does not clear a still-set allowance for more than what remains, but any next inbound funds to `I` become spendable by `C` under the stale approval).
4. Later, the same sender `S` sends a second, unrelated ICS-20 transfer (with or without a callback) over the same channel `Ch`, crediting isolated address `I` (still reused because of the deterministic derivation) with new tokens.
5. Contract `C` calls `transferFrom(I, C, newAmount)` using the still-valid stale allowance from step 2, extracting the new packet's funds without ever being re-approved by the current packet, resulting in unauthorized fund extraction from the isolated address.
